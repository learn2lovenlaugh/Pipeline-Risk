Pipeline risk plot — leak and rupture

Enter what you know about each line, from ILI, from leak history, or from an estimate. The tool converts it to an annual probability of failure, splits leak from rupture, prices both, and plots them against your acceptance limits.

Start here If this is your first time

What to gather, and what you get back

You do not need to understand the maths to get a usable answer. You do need four things per line type.

1

What the pipe is

Diameter, wall thickness, steel grade and the pressure it normally runs at. From the line list. Grade converts to SMYS: Gr B is 35,000 psi, X42 is 42,000, X52 is 52,000, X60 is 60,000.

2

Some evidence of condition

Any one of three will do. An inspection result. Or a count of leaks with the mileage and number of years that count covers. Or, if you have neither, a corrosion rate and the age of the line. Pick whichever you actually have — the tool changes its maths to suit.

3

What a failure would cost

Roughly, in dollars: cleaning up what escapes, the production you lose while it is down, and the repair. Once for a small leak and once for a full rupture. Rough is fine; order of magnitude is what matters.

4

Your limits

The dollars-per-mile-per-year below which you would not act, and above which you must. If you have never set these, start with the defaults and adjust once you see where your fleet lands.

What comes back. Two charts showing every line class against your limits, a table of the numbers behind them, a ranked list of what would actually reduce the risk on each line, and a full worked calculation for any line you pick, so you can show anyone exactly where a number came from.
Basis How the numbers are produced

How this works

Open any heading for the detail. None of it is needed to use the tool.

The short version

Corrosion does not fail a pipe on schedule. It fails at the worst spot. A rate of 35 mpy is an average across the line, and the line fails when its worst location arrives, not when the average does. Everything else follows from that.

Probability of failure is the chance that the worst feature crosses its limit during the next twelve months, given that you do not know exactly how fast the metal is going away or exactly how deep it is today. Risk is that probability multiplied by what the failure costs. Two plots, because a leak and a rupture on the same pipe are usually an order of magnitude apart in cost.

Why leak and rupture are separate

A small pit eats through the wall and the line leaks. A long stretch of thinning loses so much strength that the pipe splits open at its normal operating pressure. Which one happens is decided by pressure, wall, diameter and grade, not by preference.

The tool solves the Modified B31G burst equation for the depth at which the line would split. If that depth is above 100% of wall, the pipe perforates before it can burst, and it cannot rupture from corrosion at all — it disappears from the rupture plot. Low pressure gathering usually lands here; high pressure trunklines usually do not.

How a corrosion rate becomes a probability

Three things are uncertain: how fast the metal is going away, how deep the deepest spot is right now, and how accurate the burst equation is. The tool draws thousands of combinations of the last two and integrates the corrosion rate analytically, which lets it resolve probabilities far smaller than a plain simulation could.

For each combination it works out the rate that would be needed to reach the limit within the year, then asks how much of the rate distribution sits above that. Average across the draws and you have the probability for one feature. Combine across features and divide by length to get a rate per mile-year.

How leak history becomes a probability

When a line class has a real leak record, that record is better evidence than any corrosion model, and the tool uses it directly: leaks divided by mile-years of exposure. No wall thickness, no growth rate, no assumption about what the inhibitor achieved.

A class with no leaks does not have a probability of zero. The tool applies a Jeffreys prior, which gives roughly one half of a leak over the exposure you actually accumulated — small, non-zero, and defensible.

Why the calculation uses random draws, and why the number has nothing to do with your fleet size

Suppose your inspection says the deepest spot is 40% of wall, and the tool is accurate to plus or minus 10%. The truth could be 32% or 48%. Suppose the corrosion rate averages 6 mpy but varies from place to place. You could pick one value for each, do one calculation, and get one answer — but that answer would pretend you know things you do not.

So instead the model tries thousands of combinations. Deep and fast. Shallow and slow. Deep and slow. Every plausible pairing, weighted by how likely it is. Then it asks: in what share of those combinations does the pipe reach its limit inside the year? That share is the probability of failure. The technique has a name, Monte Carlo, but the name matters less than the reason: it is how you get an answer that carries your uncertainty rather than hiding it.

The number of draws is a setting on the arithmetic, not a count of anything real. It is not lines, not features, not miles. A fleet of 3,000 lines and a fleet of three use exactly the same setting, because the draws are spent on one representative feature at a time. Each line class gets its own calculation.

If someone asks whether you simulated the fleet, the accurate answer is no. What you did was calculate each line class separately, allowing for the fact that the depth and the growth rate are not known exactly. That is a fair sentence to put in front of management, and it is defensible if anyone pushes on it.

What the tool is not

This is a screening and comparison tool. It works on a representative feature for each line, not on your dig list. Use it to decide where to look and what to fund. Use a full fitness-for-service assessment to decide whether a specific joint stays in the ground.

Features are treated as independent, which is conservative, because real corrosion is spatially correlated. And every number it produces should be checked against your own failure history before anyone sees it.

Reference Equations, sources and how to validate

Model equations and scientific basis

Everything the tool computes, in the form an independent reviewer would need to reproduce it by hand.

1 — Remaining strength of corroded pipe

Burst pressure of a blunt metal-loss defect, Modified B31G (the 0.85dL area method):

P_f = (2 t σ_flow / D) × [1 − 0.85 (d/t)] / [1 − 0.85 (d/t) / M]

σ_flow = SMYS + 10,000 psi
M      = √(1 + 0.6275 λ − 0.003375 λ²)   for λ ≤ 50
M      = 0.032 λ + 3.3                     for λ > 50
λ      = L² / (D t)

M is the Folias bulging factor, which accounts for the fact that a long defect in a pressurised shell bulges outward and fails at a lower pressure than a short one of the same depth. This is why length, not just depth, decides whether a line ruptures.

Setting P_f equal to operating pressure and solving for depth gives the critical depth the tool reports:

(d/t)_crit = (1 − r) / [0.85 (1 − r/M)]     where r = P_op / (2 t σ_flow / D)

If this solves above 1.0 the defect perforates before the pipe can burst, and the line is leak-only at that operating stress.

2 — Damage growth and the limit state

Depth grows linearly from the measured value at the measured corrosion rate:

d(T) = d₀ + CR · T

Limit state:   g(T) = d_crit − d(T)      failure when g ≤ 0

Two limit states run in parallel. A short pit has d_crit = 1.0 and produces a leak. A long defect has d_crit from the equation above and produces a rupture. Long is taken as L ≈ 3√(D t), which is roughly where the Folias factor stops being close to unity.

3 — Turning that into a probability

Three inputs are treated as uncertain rather than fixed:

CR  ~ lognormal      σ_ln = √(ln(1 + COV²)),  μ = ln(CR̄) − σ_ln²/2
d₀  ~ normal         σ_d = tol / 1.2816        (±tol at 80% certainty)
ψ   ~ normal         mean 1, COV as set          (burst equation error)

For a given draw of depth and burst error, the rate that would be needed to reach the limit within time T follows directly, and the share of the rate distribution above it is a closed-form lognormal tail. Averaging over draws gives the cumulative probability:

F(T) = E[ 1 − Φ( (ln CR_req − μ) / σ_ln ) ]      CR_req = (d_crit·ψ − d₀) · t · 1000 / T

Integrating the rate analytically rather than sampling it is what lets the tool resolve probabilities far below one in the number of draws. The annual conditional hazard, which is what gets plotted, is the increment over the surviving fraction:

h(T) = [ F(T+1) − F(T) ] / [ 1 − F(T) ]
4 — Combining features, threats and lines

Features are combined as an independent series system, evaluated in log space so that very small probabilities do not cancel to zero:

P_line = 1 − (1 − p_short)^n_short · (1 − p_long)^n_long
       = −expm1[ n_short·ln(1−p_short) + n_long·ln(1−p_long) ]

Corrosion is then combined with the non-corrosion background rate the same way, and divided by line length:

P_total = 1 − (1 − P_corrosion)(1 − P_background)
PoF per mile-year = P_total / L

Independence between features is conservative, because real corrosion is spatially correlated.

5 — The leak-history route

Where a class has a real failure record, no corrosion model is used at all:

λ = (n_failures + 0.5) / E          E = exposure in mile-years

The half is the Jeffreys non-informative prior for a Poisson rate. It is what stops a class with no recorded leaks reading as zero risk, and it is standard practice for zero-failure reliability data.

6 — Consequence and risk
C = V·c + Q·D·m + R

V = volume released      c = remediation cost per unit volume
Q = production deferred  D = days down       m = netback per unit
R = repair and restoration cost

Risk per mile-year   = PoF × C
Fleet risk per year  = PoF × C × (number of lines) × (length each)

Everything is priced in dollars because that is the only common unit in which a gas line and an oil line can be compared. Leak and rupture are priced separately and never summed into a single consequence.

How to validate this model

Verify the arithmetic first. Reproduce the critical depth for one line by hand from the equations above, or against any independent Modified B31G calculator. The audit panel below prints every intermediate value for exactly this purpose. This step proves the code is right; it says nothing about whether the model is right.

Then hindcast. This is the evidence that convinces people. Rebuild the inputs as they stood at a past date, predict failures over the period since, and compare with what actually happened. Report predicted against observed as a ratio. Anything within a factor of two or three is credible for this class of model; further out, apply the ratio as a bias factor or disclose it.

Test ranking separately from magnitude. These are different questions and a model can pass one and fail the other. Even if the absolute numbers are off by five, the tool is useful if it puts the lines that fail near the top. Take your actual failures and ask what share of them came from the worst 20 percent of ranked mileage. If failures are spread evenly across the ranking, the model is not discriminating and the ranking should not drive spending. For a fleet-ranking tool this test matters more than absolute calibration.

Validate the inputs, not just the output. Depth calls should be checked against field measurement on dig verifications, per API 1163. If the tool is not meeting its stated tolerance, the depth uncertainty entered here is wrong and every probability downstream is wrong with it.

Find out what actually drives the answer. Vary each input across its plausible range and watch what moves. If the result is governed by a number nobody has confidence in, that is where the next data spend belongs — not wherever the risk happens to be highest.

Benchmark externally. Compare the fleet-wide computed failure rate against PHMSA incident rates per mile-year for the same commodity and class. Gathering lines were historically under-reported, so treat this as a sanity band rather than a target.

Be honest about what cannot be validated. With twenty or thirty leaks a year across the fleet, the leak side can be tested statistically within a few years. The rupture side may have no events in the entire history of the asset, and no amount of data will validate it empirically — there, all you can do is verify the equations, benchmark the inputs, and say so. The consequence side is the same: too few events to validate against outcomes, so treat it as a documented and agreed assumption set that is sensitivity-tested, never as a measured quantity.
